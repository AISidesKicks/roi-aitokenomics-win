AI Tokenomics · Field Report

Uber burned its entire 2026 AI coding budget in four months. That is not the story.

The right frame: agent adoption outran measurement and optimization on one of the world’s most complex internal infrastructures — an in-house “internal AWS” Uber ran for a decade. Uber’s own engineers report total spend “relatively stabilized since April” — the same month the budget ran dry — while usage grew 7×.

Published September 2026 · A case study for the AI Tokenomics thesis: moving from simplistic token counting to per-task cost and scaling projections.

§0The right frame

  1. What happened: Fortune reported on May 26, 2026 that Uber burned through its entire 2026 budget for AI coding tools in roughly four months — after leadership encouraged adoption by ranking teams on an internal leaderboard of total AI tool usage (Fortune).
  2. Why it is not an “AI waste” story: the burn is what happens when adoption outruns measurement. Uber’s president and COO Andrew Macdonald conceded the link between usage and outcomes — like “producing like 25% more useful consumer features” — is “not there yet” (Fortune, Rapid Response). The problem was attribution, not affordability.
  3. Why Uber burns tokens like few others: a decade of self-run infrastructure (own data centers, in-house Schemaless storage, thousands of microservices), hundreds of millions of lines of code, 50–70K tokens of MCP schema overhead per session, and engineers running 3–5 agents in parallel (Pragmatic Engineer).
  4. How it resolved: on August 27, 2026 Uber’s Distinguished Engineer reported weekly active users up 7× and requests up 9.4× (Feb–Aug) while total spend has been “relatively stabilized since April”; cost per 1,000 requests down ~34% from peak, cost per session down 52% — “also a tractable engineering challenge” (Uber Engineering).

01The timeline is the story

Four months of burn, then three months of repair. Watch April: two documents, written four months apart, point at the same month from opposite sides of the finance desk.

  1. January 2026

    Uber encourages AI adoption by ranking teams on an internal leaderboard by total AI tool usage. Teams optimize the metric — and usage takes off (Fortune).

  2. March 10, 2026

    The Pragmatic Engineer publishes its deep dive into Uber’s AI development: 84% of developers are agentic users, 65–72% of IDE code is AI-generated, Claude Code usage jumped 32% → 63% between December and February, agents open 11% of PRs, and AI costs are 6× since 2024 (Pragmatic Engineer).

  3. ≈ April 2026
    The budget runs dry — and, quietly, the spend stabilizes

    Fortune later reports the entire 2026 AI coding-tools budget was exhausted after about four months (Fortune). Keep this month in mind: Uber’s own engineering blog will later say total AI spend has been “relatively stabilized since April” (Uber Engineering).

  4. May 26, 2026
    Fortune files the story; the COO concedes the gap

    President and COO Andrew Macdonald, on the Rapid Response podcast: “That link is not there yet” between usage and business outcomes (Fortune, video).

  5. June 2026
    The engineering post-mortem

    Uber’s developer-platform team presents at the Pragmatic Summit: “70% of workloads pushed in were toil”, “4 VPs landed code in 24 minutes”, the CFO “wants revenue impact rather than diff counts”, and the pattern of “plans through expensive models, execution through cheap ones” (intentlayer, video).

  6. August 27, 2026
    Uber’s own account: stabilized since April, unit costs falling

    Distinguished Engineer Uday Kiran Medisetty reports WAU up 7× and weekly agent requests up 9.4× from February to August, while total AI spend has been “relatively stabilized since April”; with the model held constant, cost per 1,000 requests is down almost 34% from its peak and cost per session down 52% from its June peak (Uber Engineering).

  7. September 2026
    Reading the two Aprils

    Finance saw the budget die in April. Engineering stabilized spend in April, then spent the summer pushing unit costs down while usage multiplied. Same month, same event, two offices — that coincidence is the whole story (this page).

02The burn

Uber burned through its entire 2026 budget for AI coding tools in roughly four months, Fortune reported — after incentivizing adoption with an internal leaderboard that ranked teams by total AI tool usage (Fortune). The leaderboard turned a measure into a target, and Goodhart’s law did the rest: when a measure becomes a target, it stops being a good measure. Teams optimized usage; nobody had wired usage to value.

That attribution gap is exactly what Macdonald admitted. The question was whether Claude Code usage could be tied to concrete outcomes — “producing like 25% more useful consumer features” — and his answer on Rapid Response was: “That link is not there yet” (Fortune; Rapid Response video).

The macro backdrop makes the panic look misplaced

Fortune’s framing sits on Gartner’s numbers: AI-agent spending of $207B in 2026, up 139% from $86.4B in 2025; inference prices roughly 90% cheaper by 2030; and agentic workflows that need far more tokens per task than chat ever did. Sam Altman’s framing of intelligence as a metered utility is becoming the industry’s mental model, and Anthropic’s shift toward usage-based pricing pushed metered costs onto every enterprise bill (Fortune).

Scale check: Uber spent $951M on R&D in Q1 2026 alone (+17% YoY), with R&D at about 3.4% of revenue in 2025 (Fortune). Against that denominator, the AI-tools budget was never the existential line item — the crisis was that nobody could attribute what the tokens bought.

Microsoft cancels direct Claude Code licenses

Microsoft discontinued employees’ direct Claude Code licenses, steering developers to its own Copilot CLI instead (The Verge) — the same quarter Uber’s story broke.

Duolingo walks back

Duolingo walked back its AI-first messaging (Fortune) — adoption targets are easy to announce, hard to live with.

The pattern

Across 2026, the industry kept learning the same lesson in public: usage is not value, and a large token bill is not a results story (Fortune).

03The internal AWS — why Uber burns tokens like few others

Uber is a token furnace because Uber is one of the world’s most complex internal software estates — and for about nine years it ran that estate on hardware it owned itself.

A decade of self-run infrastructure

Before its own data centers, Uber ran on Peak Hosting, where the vendor managed “everything but your code” on a fleet that started at about a dozen Dell PowerEdge servers. From 2014 (San Jose, SJC) through 2019 (Phoenix, PHX, with a Washington, DC site, DCA), Uber ran its own data centers with ODM hardware from Quanta, Wiwynn, and Foxconn, built in-house storage (Schemaless, its answer to Google Spanner) and its own BI stack (Dashbuilder, where a big company would have bought Looker), and glued together thousands of microservices with multi-zone and multi-region failovers. In February 2023, Thomas Kurian confirmed the Google Cloud deal, and Uber began the five-year “Crane” migration off its remaining data centers (Pragmatic Engineer, Inside Uber’s move to the Cloud).

The historical rhyme: platforms have always looked like waste

In 2014, an internal reorg email from product chief Jeff Holden split Uber’s engineering into 16 program teams and 11 platform teams, with 30–40% of engineers working on platforms — which the author calls “a massive investment” that business people instinctively resist (Pragmatic Engineer). AI developer platforms in 2026 are the same fight one layer up: infrastructure spend that finance sees as overhead until the leverage shows up.

Why a single agent session at Uber is expensive by construction

  • The estate is enormous. Hundreds of millions of code lines and thousands of tables; the Uber blog notes agents “spend most of their turns locating information rather than generating code” (Uber Engineering).
  • Tool schemas are heavy. With 100+ tools installed, standard MCP pre-loading added roughly 50K–70K tokens of schema overhead to the initial prompt — re-sent on every context turn — across a gateway with 1,000+ servers (Uber Engineering).
  • Engineers multiply sessions. They run 3–5 agents in parallel; principal engineer Ty Smith’s description of the workflow — “coffee or browse Reddit… kick off another background agent” — is a token multiplier in one sentence (Pragmatic Engineer).
  • Background agents ride Uber’s own CI. The Minion background-agent system runs on Uber’s CI with the monorepo pre-checked out; uReview does AI code review; Autocover generates about 5,000 tests a month; Shepherd drives the Java 21 migration (Pragmatic Engineer; intentlayer).
  • Grounding was missing. Uber’s fix — the AI Context Graph, 24M nodes and 80M edges across 30+ internal systems — answers a grounded query in 38 seconds that took an ungrounded agent 20 minutes and three errors before giving a wrong answer (Uber Engineering).

Change the denominator, change the company

The post’s most important move is denominating cost in outcomes: managed agents are tracked by cost per merged PR, cost per review, cost per alert — not tokens consumed (Uber Engineering). That matches what Uber’s CFO reportedly wants — revenue impact rather than diff counts (intentlayer) — and it is exactly the shift this repo’s thesis describes: from simplistic token counting to per-task cost and scaling projections.

And keep the R&D lens: $951M of Q1 2026 R&D, +17% YoY (Fortune). Any AI-tools bill that fits inside a rounding error of that number was never going to sink Uber. Not knowing what the bill bought was the actual emergency.

05How it resolved

By late August 2026, Uber’s own account of the same stack reads like a control systems report: more than 70% of pull requests attributed to local or cloud agents; 3,600+ agent skills built across the SDLC with 30K+ executions per day; WAU up 7× and weekly agent requests up 9.4× from February to August — while total AI spend has been “relatively stabilized since April” (Uber Engineering).

The visibility layer is deliberately mundane: a live cost counter in the harness status line, a single shared spend tier across interactive harnesses, Slack nudges at 50/80/100% of expected spend, and a session-analysis dashboard that flags 16 anti-patterns with their dollar impact (Uber Engineering). Meanwhile the workload mix explains why the spend was defensible all along: “70% of workloads pushed in were toil” — upgrades, migrations, bug fixes — meaning the boring work got cheap — and the summit summary reported “4 VPs landed code in 24 minutes” (intentlayer; Pragmatic Engineer).

The April coincidence, made explicit

Fortune says the 2026 AI coding-tools budget was gone by roughly April. Uber’s engineering blog says spend has been “relatively stabilized since April” (Fortune; Uber Engineering).

Read together, the burn and the fix are the same event seen from two offices. Finance watched the budget die in April; engineering stabilized spend in April, then spent May–August driving cost per 1,000 requests down 34% and cost per session down 52% while usage grew 7×. The leaderboard found the ceiling; the platform team rebuilt the floor under it.

The verdict, from the source

“Managing and curbing rising AI coding expenses is also a tractable engineering challenge.” — Uday Kiran Medisetty, Distinguished Engineer, Uber (Running a Software Factory Efficiently at Uber Scale, Aug 27, 2026)

Not a reason to stop spending. An engineering problem with a backlog, a dashboard, and a cost equation.

06Coda: more work, not less

The economics here cut against both easy narratives. Agents did not eliminate engineering work at Uber — they created more of it: over 70% of PRs are now agent-attributed, engineers run 3–5 sessions in parallel, and 70% of agent-pushed workloads were toil that nobody had headcount for before. Adoption expanded the amount of engineering that happens; what changed is the accounting. Once spend is denominated per merged PR rather than per token, the “budget crisis” of April 2026 turns into an optimization backlog with a six-term equation attached.

That is the transferable lesson of this story, and the reason this page sits in the AI Tokenomics collection: the industry is moving from simplistic token counting to per-task cost and scaling projections — and the companies that figure that out first get to spend more, not less.
